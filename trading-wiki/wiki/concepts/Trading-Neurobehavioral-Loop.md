# The Neurobehavioural Loop

## Definition

A model of *why* traders repeat mistakes they can already name. The claim is that poor
trading decisions are not failures of character but the predictable output of a
threat-response system that runs faster than deliberation and is **reinforced** every time
it wins.

The practical consequence is the part that matters:

> **Willpower is not the missing variable.** "If you don't know how to swim and I throw
> you in a pool where you can't touch the bottom, is discipline and an iron will going to
> stop you from drowning?"
> — [[sources/YT-Pro-Trader-Amygdala-Options-Philosophy]]

Telling a trader to be more disciplined prescribes *more of the faculty that is already
losing the race*. The alternative is to change the environment so that the fast system has
less to decide.

## The loop

1. **Threat detection precedes thought.** The amygdala fires below conscious awareness. Its
   reward function is *immediate action, urgency, escape* — which is close to the exact
   inverse of what markets pay for. Good trading rewards patience and inaction.
2. **The action is taken, then justified.** Cutting a winner early *is* threat diffusion:
   the trade could turn, so closing it removes the threat. "The market's overbought" is
   written afterwards by the conscious mind. The amygdala "doesn't care about any of that."
3. **Dopamine closes the loop.** Dopamine rewards **anticipation**, not happiness. The
   anticipated relief of acting delivers the hit — so the behaviour you are trying to
   extinguish is the one being reinforced.
4. **The super-ego charges interest.** Afterwards comes *"you idiot, you knew better"*,
   which adds self-imposed pressure and urgency to the next identical situation, making the
   next firing more likely, not less.

**Overtrading falls straight out of this.** Take a loss → want to offset it → in a position
before you noticed deciding. It is loss aversion at peak, not weakness. And it rests on an
illusion of control: you control size, direction and instrument, but *the only large lever
you hold is whether to take the trade at all.*

## What actually works — remove the decision, not the emotion

The stated goal is **not** emotional flatness: *"the emotions don't go away. You just don't
let the emotions dictate the behaviour."*

- **Pre-commitment.** Every decidable thing — size, risk, invalidation, target logic —
  settled *before* entry, because the decision taken mid-cortisol is not really yours. The
  challenge is aimed at scalpers in particular: how often is that genuinely true for you?
- **Friction devices.** A deliberate ~15-second visualisation of flipping a safety catch
  before being *permitted* to look for trades. Intentionally slow. "I can't take risk, I
  haven't flipped the switch yet." Cheap, and available to anyone today.
- **Structural removal of urgency.** Choosing instruments whose payoff shape doesn't force
  action — the argument for options as a *psychological* instrument, not just a
  mathematical one (see the source page, §6). A binary "X before Y" bet manufactures the
  paralysis of watching a winner round-trip to entry.
- **Reducing what rides on the outcome.** Scarcity is an amplifier: *"most traders trade
  like they've got everything to lose, because they do."* See
  [[concepts/Capital-Management-Methods]] for the sizing arithmetic that makes a losing
  streak survivable rather than existential.
- **Physiology as a signal, not an enemy.** Heart rate is measured while trading (resting
  low-40s → 120 under load). Elevated readings are treated as **information**: my threat
  system is firing, *and so is everyone else's* — what mistakes are they about to make?

## The benevolent super-ego

The super-ego is assembled in early childhood out of adult authority voices, and its job is
to be harsh. The reform target is a **benevolent super-ego** whose post-trade review asks
only:

> Did you follow your process? Did you stick to your predetermined risk parameters?
> **Would you take this trade again?**

If the answer is no: forgive, move on, and understand precisely why the feeling is there.
No rush to congratulate on a win either — outcome is not the input.

**Three independent sources in this vault converge on this same rule**, which is a stronger
warrant than any of them alone:
- the **good-loss / bad-loss** split, where a *winning* trade taken out-of-process is still
  a bad trade ([[sources/YT-Chris-Kmer-World-Cup-Order-Flow]]);
- the **benevolent super-ego** above;
- **"a bad trade with a good outcome"** — the Brexit verdict from a prop-firm owner: great
  P&L, no plan, so what conversation would we be having if it had gone the other way?

## Common mistakes

- **Prescribing discipline.** See above. It targets the slow system and ignores the fast one.
- **Trying to stop feeling.** The goal is decoupling emotion from *behaviour*, not
  suppressing emotion. Suppression is a further tax on the same depleted faculty.
- **Hiding the P&L.** Common advice, and disputed here: switching the display to ticks
  builds "a synthetic buffer" against a discomfort you eventually have to be able to hold.
  Recorded as a genuine disagreement, not a settled point — the counter-case is that
  beginners benefit from the buffer precisely because they cannot yet hold it.
- **Assuming the deep work ends.** The source describes *currently* being unable to size
  back up after a strong start to the year, and not yet knowing why.
- **Treating the model as literal neurology** — see below.

## How much to trust the neuroscience

The "amygdala hijack" is a **popularisation** (Goleman), not a clean account of brain
architecture: the amygdala is not a discrete threat CPU and the prefrontal cortex is not
simply "the rational chip". Dopamine-as-anticipation is closer to the research
(reward-prediction error). Freud's id/ego/super-ego is a framework, not a finding, and
three years of analysis at 4–5 sessions a week is an enormous commitment with no
controlled evidence behind it.

**None of the prescriptions depend on the model being exact.** Pre-commitment, friction
devices, decoupling outcome from self-evaluation, and reducing what rides on any one trade
all stand on their own behavioural evidence. Use the model as a *serviceable metaphor for
why you keep doing the thing you can already name* — and drop it the moment it starts being
cited as a mechanism rather than a story.
